Fundamentals / four comparisons

The same idea, from four angles

Every one of these is a trade-off, not a winner. Each card puts both sides next to each other and closes with what actually follows from the difference — because the useful part is knowing when to reach for which, not which one is "better".

01 · Web2 vs Web3 02 · Ethereum vs Bitcoin 03 · Public vs private key 04 · Blockchain vs database

01 Web2 vs Web3

Who holds the data

Web2

  • Your account lives on a company's servers, and the company decides what it means.
  • Login is a username and password the platform can reset, suspend, or delete.
  • Apps are fast and cheap because one operator controls the whole stack.
  • Rules can change overnight — an API you built on can close without warning.

Web3

  • Your balances and ownership live on a shared ledger no single company controls.
  • Login is a keypair you hold; nobody can reset it, including you.
  • Apps are slower and costlier because thousands of machines verify the same work.
  • Rules are contract code — public, permanent, and hard to change unilaterally.

What follows: Web3 buys you credible neutrality and pays for it in speed, cost, and responsibility. That trade is worth it when the thing at stake is ownership or money — and usually isn't for a blog or a photo feed.

02 Ethereum vs Bitcoin

What the chain is for

Bitcoin

  • Built to be sound money: move and store value, resist change.
  • Deliberately limited scripting — fewer features, fewer ways to break.
  • Proof-of-work: security comes from spent electricity and hash power.
  • Fixed supply cap of 21 million BTC, with issuance halving on schedule.

Ethereum

  • Built to be a programmable settlement layer: run applications, not just payments.
  • Turing-complete smart contracts in the EVM — lending, DEXs, NFTs, rollups.
  • Proof-of-stake since The Merge: security comes from staked ETH that can be slashed.
  • No fixed cap; issuance is low and a portion of fees is burned under EIP-1559.

What follows: Bitcoin optimises for not changing; Ethereum optimises for what you can build. Arbitrum only makes sense on a chain of the second kind — a rollup needs a programmable base layer to verify its proofs.

03 Public key vs private key

How ownership is proven

Public key

  • Derived from the private key, and your address is derived from it in turn.
  • Safe to share — it is how people send you funds and how anyone identifies you on-chain.
  • Used by the network to verify that a signature really came from you.
  • Reveals your entire transaction history, since the ledger is public.

Private key

  • A secret number generated first; everything else is computed from it.
  • Never shared. Whoever holds it owns the funds — there is no appeal above it.
  • Used to sign transactions, proving authorisation without exposing the key.
  • Cannot be recovered if lost, and cannot be revoked if stolen.

What follows: The maths runs one way only — public from private, never the reverse. That asymmetry is the whole security model, which is why a seed phrase in a screenshot is the most common way people lose everything. The Connect wallet button in the bar above is this in practice: the site receives an address your wallet chose to reveal and can do nothing else with it. It never sees, asks for, or can derive the private key behind it.

04 Blockchain vs traditional database

Who can rewrite history

Traditional database

  • An administrator can UPDATE or DELETE any row, and often nobody outside would know.
  • One owner, one source of truth, trusted because of the institution behind it.
  • Extremely fast and cheap — millions of writes a second is ordinary.
  • Optimised for correct, current state. History is a backup, not a guarantee.

Blockchain

  • Append-only. Each block commits to the hash of the one before it, so edits cascade.
  • Replicated across many independent nodes that must agree on the same history.
  • Slow and expensive by design — redundancy is the product, not the overhead.
  • Optimised for verifiable history. Anyone can re-check every state change from genesis.

What follows: Use a blockchain only when the cost of someone silently editing the record is higher than the cost of the redundancy. See it happen on the Block Simulator — change one block's data and every block after it turns invalid.

Recap

If you only remember one line per comparison

A one-sentence summary of each of the four comparisons
Comparison The real difference When it matters
Web2 vs Web3 Whether a company or a keypair decides what your account means. Any time ownership or money is at stake rather than content.
Ethereum vs Bitcoin Programmable settlement layer vs deliberately minimal sound money. Choosing a base layer to build on — rollups need the programmable kind.
Public vs private key One verifies signatures in public; the other creates them in secret. Every wallet decision you will ever make, including backups.
Blockchain vs database Append-only history anyone can re-check vs editable state one owner controls. When silent edits to the record would be worse than the extra cost.
