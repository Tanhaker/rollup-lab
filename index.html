The case for Layer 2

Three moves that took Ethereum from congested to affordable

Read these in order — each one only makes sense because of the one before it.

Why Ethereum needed Layer 2

Every Ethereum node re-executes and stores every transaction. That redundancy is exactly what makes the chain trustworthy, and it is also a hard ceiling on throughput: block space is scarce and sold by auction. When demand spikes — an NFT mint, a market crash — the auction price of gas spikes with it, and a simple token swap can cost more than the thing being swapped. Ethereum could raise the ceiling by demanding bigger, more expensive nodes, but that pushes ordinary people out of running one and centralises the network. Layer 2 is the other way out: move execution off-chain, keep verification on-chain.

What Arbitrum is and how it helps

Arbitrum One is an optimistic rollup. It runs its own EVM-compatible execution environment where transactions are cheap and fast, then compresses batches of them and posts the data back to Ethereum. "Optimistic" means the batch is assumed correct by default; anyone watching can challenge it during a dispute window, and Arbitrum's interactive fraud proof narrows the disagreement down to a single instruction that Ethereum itself can adjudicate. So the honest party wins as long as at least one honest party is watching — Arbitrum inherits Ethereum's security rather than inventing its own validator set.

One real-world benefit over mainnet

Cost per transaction. Because hundreds of user transactions share the cost of one compressed posting to Ethereum, a transfer or swap on Arbitrum typically costs a small fraction of the same action on mainnet — and since EIP-4844 gave rollups a cheaper data lane (blobs), that gap widened again. Concretely: a payment that isn't worth making at a few dollars of mainnet gas becomes worth making at a few cents. That is the difference between a chain used for settlement and a chain people can actually use. You don't have to take that on trust — the Live Prices page reads both gas prices straight off the two chains and puts them side by side.

What makes it work

Three properties worth understanding

Data goes back to Ethereum

A rollup doesn't ask you to trust it. The transaction data needed to rebuild L2 state is published to Ethereum, so anyone can reconstruct the chain and check it independently. Availability of that data is what separates a rollup from a sidechain.

Fraud proofs, not blind trust

Batches are posted optimistically and can be challenged during the dispute window. Arbitrum runs an interactive, multi-round bisection game that isolates the exact disputed step, so Ethereum only has to verify one tiny instruction instead of re-running everything.

The same tools you already know

Arbitrum's Nitro stack targets EVM equivalence, so Solidity contracts, MetaMask, Hardhat, Foundry and existing tooling work with minimal changes. Cheap block space is only useful if developers can actually reach it.

End to end

What happens to one transaction on Arbitrum

The hero animation above compresses this into a loop. Here it is spelled out, in the order a single transaction experiences it.

1 · Submit

You sign a transaction with your private key and send it to the Arbitrum sequencer instead of the Ethereum mempool.

2 · Order & execute

The sequencer fixes the ordering and runs it through Nitro's EVM. You get a soft confirmation in well under a second.

3 · Compress & post

Your transaction is bundled with hundreds of others, compressed, and posted to Ethereum — one L1 cost shared by everyone in the batch.

4 · Settle

The state root is asserted on L1 and becomes final once the challenge window passes with no successful fraud proof.

Under the hood

Which parts of this site touch a real blockchain

Plenty of sites explain blockchains without ever talking to one. Here is exactly where the line falls on this site, so you can check the claim rather than believe it.

Real — the network rail

The bar at the top of every page is a live JSON-RPC connection to an Arbitrum Sepolia node. The block height ticking up is that chain advancing. Nothing about it is simulated.

Real — the wallet

Connect a wallet and the site calls eth_requestAccounts and eth_getBalance over EIP-1193. Read-only: it never asks you to sign anything, and it cannot see your private key.

Simulated — the mining page

The Block Simulator hashes in your browser with the Web Crypto API. The SHA-256 is real, the chain is not — it is a teaching model with no network or consensus. The page says so, and shows real Arbitrum blocks beside it for comparison.
